Where To Sleep Tonight

The Safe & Legal Parking Toolkit

*A free bonus for readers of* Van Life for Beginners *(Open Road Publishing)*

Finding a safe, legal, quiet place to sleep is the single biggest daily stressor in van life — and the fastest way to a knock on the window at 2 a.m. This toolkit gives you a repeatable system so you never have to guess. Read it once at home. Then keep it on your phone.

The one rule that keeps you out of trouble: *legal first, safe second, comfortable third.* If a spot fails "legal," skip it — a ticket, a tow, or a "move along" ruins a week of savings and sleep.

Google Maps satellite: your free scouting tool

Every pin you find should get a 60-second satellite check before you drive an hour to reach it:

  • Switch to Satellite view and zoom in on the exact coordinates. You're looking for a real pull-off, a widened shoulder, or an existing bare/disturbed clearing — not a spot you'd have to create.
  • Check the approach road. A thin, pale, wandering line often means dirt/sand/washboard. A gate symbol or a cattle-guard shadow can mean "locked at dusk." Trace the road back to pavement so you know your escape route.
  • Look for red flags nearby: a house or ranch within sight (that's someone's backyard), "No Trespassing" implied by fence lines, a wash/dry riverbed (flash-flood death trap — never camp in one), or a slope with no flat ground.
  • Use Street View on the nearest paved road to read any posted signs ("No Overnight Parking," "Day Use Only," "Permit Required") before you commit.
  • Measure it: click two points to confirm you're within the legal distance of the road (see Part 2) and that the clearing is actually van-sized and level.

Part 2 — Free public land: BLM & National Forest dispersed camping

This is the heart of affordable van life: on most Bureau of Land Management (BLM) land and in most National Forests, "dispersed camping" (camping outside a developed campground) is free and legal. It's also where the rules matter most, because rangers do enforce them.

The core rules (accurate US federal-land basics)

  • 14-day stay limit. The near-universal rule is a maximum of 14 days within a 28-day period on BLM land (National Forests are typically 14 days at a time, sometimes within a 30-day window). After that you must move on — often 25 miles or more — not just shuffle down the road. Limits vary by field office/ranger district, and popular areas post shorter limits.
  • Stay in the corridor, on already-used ground. With a vehicle you generally must camp within about 150 feet of a designated open route (some forests allow a 100–300 ft "camping corridor" from the road centerline; others less). Park on bare, previously-disturbed ground — never drive off onto vegetation to make a new spot.
  • Keep your distance from water. Camp at least 100–200 feet (roughly 70+ steps) from any stream, lake, or spring, trail, or road. This protects the water and wildlife that need to reach it.
  • The MVUM is the law of the forest. In a National Forest, the Motor Vehicle Use Map (MVUM) is the authoritative document showing which roads are legally open to your vehicle and where you may drive off-road to camp. MVUMs are free — download them from the specific forest's page on fs.usda.gov, or load the georeferenced version in the free Avenza Maps app so your blue GPS dot shows on the official map even with no signal. For BLM, check the field-office page or call ahead.
  • Fire before anything. Check the current fire restrictions / stage bans for that exact unit before you even think about a fire or, in extreme stages, running a generator. Bans change weekly in summer.

Leave No Trace (this is what keeps land open)

Every spot that gets shut down was shut down because someone trashed it. Follow the seven principles — the ones that matter most for van life:

  1. Pack it in, pack it out — *all* trash, including food scraps and toilet paper.
  2. Human waste: use a real system (composting/cassette toilet, or a WAG bag) or dig a 6–8 inch cathole at least 200 feet from water. Never leave TP.
  3. Camp on durable, already-used surfaces. Don't widen the clearing.
  4. Grey water: scatter it well away from water sources; strain food bits out first.
  5. Leave it better than you found it — pick up the previous camper's micro-trash. Rangers notice clean camps and clean campers get left alone.

The long-stay exception

In parts of the desert Southwest, BLM runs Long-Term Visitor Areas (LTVAs) — e.g., around Quartzsite, AZ — where a cheap seasonal permit (about $180 for the season or ~$40 for two weeks) legally lets you stay for months. This is the winter home base for a huge slice of the van/RV community.

The Walmart / retailer ask script

Walk in, find a manager (not a cashier), smile, and say:

> *"Hi — I'm passing through tonight in my van. Is it okay if I park at the far end of your lot for one night and head out early in the morning? I'll keep to myself and buy a few things while I'm here."*

If yes, thank them and do exactly that. If no, thank them anyway and move on — a manager who said no will remember a van that stayed. Never argue; never assume silence is a yes.

Part 4 — The city-ordinance reality (read this before any urban night)

Here's what most free blogs won't tell you plainly: a large and growing number of US cities legally ban "overnight vehicle habitation" — sleeping in your vehicle — regardless of whose parking lot you're in. Violations range from a warning to a ticket ($50–$100+) to, in some cities, a tow. The retailer's permission does not override a city ordinance.

How to check a city's rules (5 minutes, do it before dark)

  • Search: [city name] municipal code "vehicle habitation" or [city] ordinance sleeping in car overnight or [city] oversized vehicle parking ordinance. City codes are public and usually hosted on municode.com or the city's own site.
  • Look for these trigger words: *vehicle habitation, camping in vehicle, oversized vehicle, overnight on-street parking, 72-hour rule.*
  • Call the non-emergency police line and ask directly: *"Is it legal to sleep in a legally parked vehicle overnight in your city?"* They'll tell you, and now you can't be surprised.
  • Note the pattern: bans cluster in wealthy suburbs, beach/tourist towns, and downtown cores. Industrial and commercial-zoned streets, and the big-box retail strip on the edge of town, are usually your safest bet.

Stealth basics (for legal-gray urban nights)

Stealth isn't about hiding a crime — it's about not drawing attention so no one has a reason to call anyone. The goal is to look like a parked work van, not a campsite.

  • Arrive late, leave early. Roll in after dark (9–10 p.m.), be gone by 6–7 a.m. The less time you're there, the less risk.
  • Go dark inside. No interior lights visible from outside once parked. Cook and set up *before* you park, or use a dim red headlamp low in the van.
  • Blackout your windows completely. Custom-cut Reflectix or magnetic covers on every window, installed so no light leaks and no one can see in. A cab curtain behind the front seats hides the "someone's living back here" tell.
  • Look like a work van, not a home. No stickers, no ladder rack full of gear, no curtains flapping, nothing on the dash, cab kept tidy. A plain white van is invisible.
  • Never return to the same spot two nights running. Rotate. Familiarity is how neighbors notice you.
  • Stay quiet and self-contained. No door slams, no music, no generator, no stepping out to stretch every hour. If you must run a fan, keep it low.
  • Trust your gut and always have an exit. Back into nothing you can't pull straight out of. Keep the key in a known spot, shoes on or handy. If a place feels wrong, leave — there's always another spot.

Part 6 — The stealth / overnight checklist

Run this every time you park for the night. Print it small; laminate it if you're fancy.

Before you park

  • [ ] Checked the city ordinance (urban) or the 14-day/road-distance rule (public land)
  • [ ] Read every posted sign at this spot
  • [ ] Van is level enough to sleep (chock if needed)
  • [ ] Fuel enough to leave immediately if you must
  • [ ] Cooked / prepped so I won't need lights or the stove later

Settling in

  • [ ] Backed in or positioned for a straight, fast exit
  • [ ] All windows blacked out — no light leaks, no view in
  • [ ] Cab curtain / covers up; dash clear; looks like an empty work van
  • [ ] Interior lights off or dim red only
  • [ ] Doors locked; valuables out of sight; keys in known spot; shoes handy
  • [ ] Roof vent/fan set low; no generator running overnight

Mindset

  • [ ] I have a Plan B spot picked in case I get moved along
  • [ ] If it feels wrong, I leave — no spot is worth a bad gut feeling
  • [ ] Arrive late, leave early, don't come back tomorrow

*Rules, prices, and store policies change and vary by location. This toolkit gives you the framework and the current (2026) baseline — always verify the specific spot's posted signs, the local ordinance, and the managing agency (BLM field office or Forest Service ranger district) before you rely on it. When in doubt, ask. A five-minute question beats a midnight knock.*

Sleep safe. Sleep legal. Wake up somewhere beautiful.

Monthly Van-Life Cost + Income Reality Worksheet

Your Free Bonus from *Van Life for Beginners*

Building the van is a one-time cost. Living in it is a monthly one — and this is where the Instagram fantasy quietly falls apart. People quit their apartment picturing $600-a-month freedom, forget about fuel, insurance, and the repair fund, and drive home broke six months later.

This worksheet is the antidote. It's a real break-even tool: a full monthly cost sheet with honest 2026 US ranges, the actual income options for earning on the road (with the connectivity each one needs), and a runway calculator that tells you how much to save before you go.

How to use it: read the three example columns (Frugal / Typical / Comfortable), write your own numbers in the blank column, total your costs, total your realistic income, and take both to the Break-Even section at the end.

Part 1 — Monthly Costs

The three columns are: Frugal (slow travel, mostly free camping, subsidized or minimal health insurance), Typical (a normal balance of free and paid nights, moderate travel), and Comfortable (RV parks, faster travel, full-price health insurance).

So the honest ballpark: most solo full-timers land around $1,200–$2,000/month. You can push it under $1,000 if you're frugal, healthy or subsidized, and travel slowly. It climbs past $3,000 the moment you add regular RV parks and full-price health insurance. Couples don't double it — you split fuel, insurance, campsites, and connectivity, so two people typically run about 1.4×, not 2×, a solo budget.

Notes on the sneaky lines

  • Starlink: phone-hotspot-only is the Frugal option (~$50/mo). Add Starlink Roam and you're at ~$50/mo (regional, deprioritized) up to ~$165/mo (unlimited/priority) — that's the difference between the Typical and Comfortable connectivity columns. If your income depends on the internet, this is not the line to cut.
  • Repairs / maintenance reserve: this is the line everyone skips and everyone regrets. You are driving your house. Budget $100–400/month into a separate fund *before* anything breaks. Oil, tires, and brakes are routine; a transmission, alternator, or timing job is a $1,500–3,500 surprise that arrives with zero warning. An older Shoestring van needs the *higher* reserve, not the lower one.
  • Health insurance: the single most unpredictable line. A subsidized ACA plan for a young, low-income nomad can be $0–150; the same person unsubsidized pays $400–600+. It swings on your state of domicile, your age, and your reported income. Get real quotes at healthcare.gov for your domicile state before you go — don't guess this one.

> Reality check: the sustainable van-lifers almost all have one boring, reliable income (a remote job, a freelance pipeline, or a seasonal-work rhythm) — *plus* savings. The ones who leave on "I'll figure out money on the road" or "my channel will blow up" are the ones who quit within a year.

Step 2 — How much runway to save before you go

> Runway you'll burn through = Monthly deficit × Number of months you want covered

Even if your income fully covers your costs, do not leave with an empty savings account. Recommended minimum cushion *before* you turn the key:

Your situationMinimum before you go
Income already secured (remote job / signed freelance clients)3 months of Typical costs in cash + a $2,000–3,000 van-repair fund
Income not yet secured ("I'll find work out there")6 months of Typical costs in cash + a $2,000–3,000 van-repair fund

Worked example: costs $1,565/mo, income $1,200/mo → deficit $365/mo. With income *not* secured, you want 6 months of costs ($9,390) + a $2,500 repair fund = ~$11,900 saved before departure. If instead your income *covers* costs, you still want ~3 months ($4,695) + $2,500 = ~$7,200 so one bad month or one big repair doesn't end the trip.

The One Thing Most People Get Wrong

They budget the rent they're *escaping* and forget the costs they're *inheriting*. Three lines quietly eat the "savings" everyone expects:

  1. Fuel — it scales directly with how fast you travel. The single biggest budget fix in van life is simply slowing down: park longer, drive less, and this line can drop by half.
  2. Insurance — non-optional, every month, whether you drove or not.
  3. The repair reserve — the one nobody funds and everyone eventually needs. Your house has 150,000 miles on it.

Fund those three from day one and van life is genuinely affordable — often cheaper than an apartment. Ignore them and the "$600 a month" dream turns into a tow bill on the side of a highway. Now you know the real numbers. Go build the life — with your eyes open.
